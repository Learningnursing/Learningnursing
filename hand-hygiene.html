Hand Hygiene

A complete nursing guide to routine handwashing, alcohol-based hand rub, the WHO Five Moments, glove use and surgical hand preparation.

BeginnerStep-by-step guideEducational resource
Educational notice: This page contains educational content only. Always follow your institution's current policies, professional standards and applicable clinical guidance.

Quick overview

Hand hygiene is one of the most important infection-prevention practices in healthcare. It includes cleaning the hands with soap and water, using an alcohol-based hand rub when appropriate, and surgical hand preparation before surgical procedures. For routine handwashing, the WHO procedure takes approximately 40–60 seconds. When hands are not visibly soiled, an alcohol-based hand rub is generally preferred for routine clinical hand hygiene.

Why is it important?

Why hand hygiene matters:
Hand hygiene helps reduce the transmission of microorganisms between healthcare workers, patients and the clinical environment.

WHO Five Moments:
1. Before touching a patient
2. Before a clean/aseptic procedure
3. After body fluid exposure risk
4. After touching a patient
5. After touching patient surroundings

Important: Gloves do not replace hand hygiene. Perform hand hygiene when clinically indicated before putting on gloves and after removing them.

Step-by-step guide

1

Recognise the indication

Identify when hand hygiene is required before beginning patient care.

Use the WHO Five Moments as a framework: before touching a patient, before a clean or aseptic procedure, after body fluid exposure risk, after touching a patient and after touching patient surroundings.

2

Prepare your hands

Remove unnecessary hand and wrist jewellery and keep fingernails short and clean.

Artificial nails and extensions should be avoided when caring for high-risk patients or when required by institutional policy. Check the hands for visible contamination.

3

Wet the hands

For soap-and-water handwashing, wet the hands with clean running water.

Apply enough liquid soap to cover all surfaces of the hands.

4

Rub palm to palm

Rub the palms together.

Create a lather and begin covering all surfaces of both hands.

5

Clean the backs and between the fingers

Rub the right palm over the left dorsum with fingers interlaced, then repeat on the opposite side.

Continue by rubbing palm to palm with fingers interlaced so that the spaces between the fingers are cleaned.

6

Clean the backs of the fingers

Rub the backs of the fingers against the opposite palm with the fingers interlocked.

This helps clean areas that are commonly missed during routine hand hygiene.

7

Clean the thumbs and fingertips

Rotationally rub each thumb and clean the fingertips and nail areas against the opposite palm.

Pay particular attention to thumbs, fingertips, around and under the fingernails and other frequently missed areas.

8

Clean the wrists

Include the wrists when performing the handwashing procedure.

Ensure all required hand surfaces have been cleaned before rinsing.

9

Rinse and dry

Rinse thoroughly with clean running water and dry completely with a single-use towel.

For the WHO handwashing sequence, the entire procedure takes approximately 40–60 seconds.

10

Turn off the faucet safely

Use the disposable towel to turn off the faucet when appropriate.

This helps avoid recontaminating clean hands by touching a potentially contaminated faucet handle.

11

Alcohol-based hand rub

When hands are not visibly soiled, use an appropriate alcohol-based hand rub according to the product instructions.

Apply enough product to cover all hand surfaces. Rub palms, backs of hands, between fingers, backs of fingers, thumbs and fingertips until the hands are completely dry. The CDC describes approximately 20 seconds for this process.

12

Surgical hand preparation

Before surgery, follow the approved surgical hand preparation method and the product manufacturer's instructions.

Surgical hand preparation may use an antimicrobial soap-and-water method or an approved surgical alcohol-based hand preparation. Remove jewellery, keep nails short and clean, and follow the operating-room protocol for drying, gowning and sterile gloving. WHO guidance does not routinely recommend nail brushes because they can damage the skin.

13

Hand hygiene and gloves

Never use gloves as a substitute for hand hygiene.

Perform hand hygiene whenever indicated before donning gloves and after removing gloves. Change gloves when required by the clinical situation, including when moving from a contaminated body site to a clean body site or between patients.
